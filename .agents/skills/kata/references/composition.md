# Kata forms

A form gives a communicative relationship a spatial expression. Its name describes
what the arrangement does; columns, proportions and type sizes are ways to realize
it. These three forms are starting points for judgment, usable by people and agents.
Read the [shared practices](practices.md) for preserving content and composing its
captions, labels, qualifications, headings and footers.

- [One focus](#one-focus): give one subject presence.
- [Equal peers](#equal-peers): support comparable consideration.
- [A lead with support](#a-lead-with-support): express an authored priority.

The examples below are fictional, schematic teaching material. Within each pair,
the supplied words stay the same. Diagrams show relationships, not fixed dimensions
or a required brand. The explanations are design judgments, not measured effects.

## One focus

**Purpose.** Let one subject hold attention. A title, statement, photograph or
supplied chart can occupy a surface without additional content competing with it.

**Example brief.** An opening card for a neighbourhood reading event, using only
“A place to read together” and “Neighbourhood library”. The desired character
is welcoming and quiet.

**Arrangement A — a clear starting edge.** Give the subject a bounded text measure,
a prominent scale and a shared alignment with its context. Leave open space around
the group. Use a larger type scale for the heading than for its attribution.

```text
┌────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                                            │
│    A place to read                         │
│    together                                │
│                                            │
│    Neighbourhood library                   │
│                                            │
│                                            │
└────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

The common edge connects subject and context. Scale establishes presence;
whitespace gives that presence room. The offset creates an unequal distribution
of weight that can feel more assertive as the heading grows.

**Arrangement B — a quiet centre.** Use a more modest heading scale, centre the
group and allow generous margins. Keep the attribution subordinate and near it.

```text
┌────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                                            │
│                                            │
│              A place to read               │
│                  together                  │
│                                            │
│           Neighbourhood library            │
│                                            │
│                                            │
└────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

For this brief, B is a reasonable choice: its balanced placement and restraint
serve the requested quiet character. A offers a stronger reading edge and can
accommodate a longer subject more comfortably. Neither requires extra decoration.

**Adapt and recognize limits.** A long title may need a wider measure and less
outer space. A photograph may need its whole frame preserved; a supplied chart
needs enough area for its existing labels. Two independently important subjects
call for [equal peers](#equal-peers). Do not enlarge a heading until its necessary
qualification or attribution becomes unreadable.


## Equal peers

**Purpose.** Let subjects receive comparable consideration without implying an
unsupported preference. Equal visual weight includes scale, contrast, position
and surrounding space as well as width.

**Example brief.** Compare two workshop venues. Neither is preferred. Supplied
material: Station hall, 48 people, €240 / evening; Garden room, 32 people,
€160 / evening; the shared qualification “Provisional quotes; staffing and
insurance excluded.” The heading is “Two places for the workshop”.

**Arrangement A — fields beside each other.** Use equal widths, a common top edge
and matching title scales. Align the supplied corresponding details. Keep the
shared qualification visibly associated with both fields.

```text
┌────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│    Two places for the workshop             │
│                                            │
│    Station hall        Garden room         │
│    48 people           32 people           │
│    €240 / evening      €160 / evening      │
│                                            │
│    Provisional quotes; staffing and        │
│    insurance excluded.                     │
└────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

The gutter separates the venues; the repeated alignment makes the same kinds of
detail easy to locate. Comparable treatment keeps the choice open. The larger
capacity does not earn a larger title, badge or darker panel.

**Arrangement B — equal rows.** Stack the subjects with matching treatment and
spacing. Each keeps its own details; the shared qualification follows both.

```text
┌────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│    Two places for the workshop             │
│                                            │
│    Station hall                            │
│    48 people          €240 / evening       │
│                                            │
│    Garden room                             │
│    32 people          €160 / evening       │
│                                            │
│    Provisional quotes; staffing and        │
│    insurance excluded.                     │
└────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

For this short comparison on a wide surface, A makes simultaneous comparison
more direct. B provides longer lines when the same subjects have substantial
supplied descriptions, at the cost of more vertical reading. Equal treatment
can survive that change of geometry.

**Adapt and recognize limits.** Unequal text lengths do not justify shrinking
only one subject's text or promoting the shorter title. Allow depth or adjust
both fields consistently. Three brief peers may fit columns; longer descriptions
may need rows. Keep an authored comparison table intact and compose its surroundings.
When an explicit recommendation should lead, use [lead with support](#a-lead-with-support).


## A lead with support

**Purpose.** Make an author-supplied priority visible while keeping its context,
evidence and conditions usable. A photograph can lead its caption, a statement
its evidence, or an explicit recommendation its supporting material.

**Example brief.** Present a planned launch date with its condition. Supplied
words: “Planned launch”, “18 June”, “Security review due 10 June.” and “Planned,
not confirmed.” The date should lead, while its provisional status remains clear.

**Arrangement A — unequal fields.** Give the date more area and a larger scale.
Keep its status immediately below it. Align the support field with the date group
so the security review remains part of the same reading unit.

```text
┌────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│    Planned launch                          │
│                                            │
│    18 June                Security review  │
│    Planned,               due 10 June.     │
│    not confirmed.                          │
│                                            │
└────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

The unequal space and scale establish the lead. Proximity keeps the qualification
attached to the date. The shared top alignment connects the supporting review.
Try roughly two parts of the width for the lead and one for support, then adjust
for actual content. This proportion does not encode a quantity or prove priority.

**Arrangement B — lead above support.** Use the reading direction to connect the
date, its status and the review. Distinguish them through scale and spacing.

```text
┌────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│    Planned launch                          │
│                                            │
│    18 June                                 │
│    Planned, not confirmed.                 │
│                                            │
│    Security review due 10 June.            │
│                                            │
└────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

A gives the date and review a side-by-side relationship. B provides a more direct
reading sequence and longer lines for support. For a brief where the condition
must be read with the date, B is a reasonable starting choice. In either case,
“Planned, not confirmed” stays legible beside the claim it qualifies.

**Adapt and recognize limits.** Choose proportions for the actual visual weight.
A dark image may dominate a similarly sized text field. Golden proportions are
one possible choice, with no privileged status. If the support becomes cramped,
reduce the lead's allocation or move support below it. When no priority is
supplied, consider [equal peers](#equal-peers); never invent a recommendation to
justify an asymmetric layout.
